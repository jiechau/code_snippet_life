AstroScore daily full

astro-score_daily.html with the sky's own calendar under it. The next 16 days for every saved spot at once: a row per place, a column per day, and each cell two block glyphs, one per half-day. Same AstroScore (觀星 score) as astro-score_readable.html — this page just asks it of every place in the list at once (10 rows) and folds each day's 24 hours into two half-day blocks. What the daily grid adds here is four rows of pure geometry under each bar: three digits giving how high a body got in the dark that half-day, in tens of degrees — purple for A* (銀心), orange for M42, slate for the moon (6 = reached the sixties, blank = never cleared 10°) — and the moon phase (🌑 … 🌕 … 🌘) under the cell. The slate digit is the one that means the opposite of the others: high is bad. None of the three depends on the forecast, so they stay true to the far end of the grid where the cloud models have given out. The first row, 輸入, is the one that is not a saved spot: it follows the location box below, which 使用目前位置 fills from the device and 在地圖上點選 from a map. Read it to pick where and when, then open the readable grid for the hour-by-hour detail. Four forecast models come back in every request; the tabs pick which one the bars are scored from. No API key needed.

  • AstroScore (觀星) says the sky is dark, moonless and clear but knows nothing about where A* is; the core's altitude says the subject is up but knows nothing about the sky. Only both at once colour the strip, so a green bar with no purple under it is a fine night with the core down.
  • It is in degrees, so its ceiling is the core's own transit altitude — ~35.8° at the northernmost saved spot (大武崙砲台) to ~39.1° at the southernmost (龍磐公園) over Taiwan. The top two shades belong to lower latitudes.

  • The lower strip is the Orion Nebula (M42) read exactly as the purple one reads Sgr A* (銀心), on the same ladder, so the two can be compared directly: whichever is deeper tonight is the target to point at.
  • They rarely paint together, which is the point of having both, and the grid shows it twice over. Across the year: A* (RA 17h45m) is a summer object from Taiwan and M42 (RA 05h35m) a winter one, so for half the year the purple strip is blank on every row and a clear night has nothing under it — the orange strip is what that half looks like. And within a single autumn night the two fall in opposite halves: the core is up in the evening and gone by the small hours, M42 the other way about. So the purple tends to sit under a day's right glyph and the orange under the next day's left one — the same one-night-across-two-cells reading as the bars, now saying which target each half of the night belongs to.
  • At Dec −5° rather than −29°, M42 rides far higher here — transiting about +59° to +63° over Taiwan — so unlike the purple ramp, the deepest orange is reachable from these spots. A saturated orange strip is a genuinely excellent M42 night, not a scale running out.

  • The marks are geometry, with no weather in them. The strips above weigh the sky and the subject together, so they go blank on a cloudy night; a digit reports only how high the subject got while the sun was down, which is as certain at day 16 as at day 1. So a digit can stand under a blank bar — the subject was up, the sky was hopeless — or under a no-forecast ·, and that is exactly what they are for: when is the season, read separately from which night is clear. Where a strip is coloured its digit is there too; the interesting cells are the ones with a digit and no strip.
  • The third digit is the moon, and it means the opposite of the two above it. Same rule, same tens, inverted reading: a high purple or orange digit is a reason to drive out, a high slate one is the reason not to. Nothing in the code tells them apart — that is deliberate, they are the same measurement — so the colour is what carries it, cool and dark where the two targets are warm. Its gate is the same 10°, which here is also where MoonPenalty (月亮扣分) tops out, so any slate digit at all means at least one dark hour of that block scored zero on moonlight alone.
  • The moon digit is the block's worst, the bar is its best, and both are true: a block can carry a slate 8 and still show a tall bar, when the good hour was one the moon had not risen for. That pairing is the useful one — it is a moonless window inside a moonlit night. The slate row emptying out while the digit above it stays high (the moon rising later each night until the evening half is clear of it) is the same story read along the row.
  • Read them down a column as well as across a row. A digit sliding 6 6 6 5 5 4 along a row is a target going out of season week by week, which a plain presence mark could not show; and the rows separate at a glance, since over Taiwan M42 marks in the 5s and 6s where A* marks in the 2s and 3s.
  • Tens only, never two digits — the mark shares the one character the bar above it is wide, and 10° of resolution is already finer than the question deserves: 61° against 68° changes no decision, 61° against 21° changes which target you point at. Hover a cell for all three exact altitudes.
  • 10° rather than the horizon, because below that you are looking through more than five atmospheres of haze and most real horizons have a ridge or a tree line in them. It is the same number as MoonPenalty (月亮扣分) tops out at, by coincidence and not for the same reason.
  • Dark is the page's own −10° sun threshold — the one AstroScore zeroes the score above — so a mark and the bar over it agree about when the night starts.
  • The altitude a digit rounds down is the highest the subject got while it was dark, which is not its transit altitude: in late September A* transits at 龍磐公園 around +39° but does it in twilight, so the marks there read 2 and 3 for +29° to +36° rather than the 3 the ceiling alone would give every night. A target whose whole passage falls in daylight is correctly unmarked however high it climbs.
  • The moon glyph is per day, not per half-day, unlike everything above it: the elongation moves about 12° in 24 hours — 14.3° at its fastest — which is under a third of the 45° each glyph covers, so the two halves of a date are the same phase but for the odd boundary. It is drawn for local noon, where the two blocks meet.
  • It is a reminder rather than an input: AstroScore already docks the score for moon altitude and knows nothing of the phase, so the glyph is what tells a bright bar under a 🌕 from one under a 🌑. A full moon that has not risen yet costs nothing; the same moon at 10° costs everything. The phase and the slate digit are the two halves of that: how bright the moon is, and how high it got — a 🌕 over a blank slate row is the good case, a 🌕 over a slate 8 the bad one. Hover a cell for the phase name and the elongation behind the glyph.
  • Each block is half a day, so each holds exactly one dark stretch: the small hours fall in 00–11 and the evening in 12–23, while their daylight halves score 0 and cannot touch a maximum.
  • A night straddles midnight, so it reads across two cells — the right glyph of one day (that evening) and the left glyph of the next (its small hours). █ on the right of 08/18 next to █ on the left of 08/19 is one clear night. A single cell showing ██ is instead the tail of the night just gone beside the head of the one coming.
  • The best hour, not a total or an average — one clear hour is worth the drive even when the rest of the night clouds over, and a sum would average it away. It also means a bar says nothing about how long the window lasts: open the readable grid for that.
  • Hide past days (從今天開始) is on to start with, so the page opens on today and the days ahead. Untick it to bring back the week past_days asked for: a forecast beside what the sky actually did, with a heavier rule marking today. It drops whole days only, and never shortens a day it shows — today's column always covers the full 24 hours, hours already gone included, so a block does not shrink as the afternoon wears on. Hover any cell for the exact sums.
  • The score itself is (100 − Cloud) × (100 − MoonPenalty) / 100, zeroed while the sun is above −10°, MoonPenalty (月亮扣分) ramping 0 → 100 as the moon climbs 0° → 10°. Sun and moon are computed in the page. See astro-score_readable.html for the worked examples.
  • The models do not all reach 16 days out, and here that reads as a blank, not a dash: icon_global stops after about 7.5 days, jma_seamless after 11 and ecmwf_ifs025 after 15, while gfs_global fills all 16. Beyond its own horizon a model returns nothing and no hour is scored, which is not the same answer as a hopeless night — so those blocks draw a faint · rather than the blank a washed-out sky gets. Expect a field of them at the right end of the grid; hover for the (0/12 hr) count behind it.
  • Cloud (雲量) comes from one model at a time, picked with the cloud source (雲量來源) tabs above the grid. All four are fetched in the same request, so switching is instant and costs nothing — and disagreement between them is information: where jma and icon tell the same story the forecast is worth trusting, and where they do not, it is not. The default is ecmwf_ifs025; jma_seamless is the one to switch to for a spot in the Central Range, being the only one of the four with a ~5 km regional nest over Taiwan rather than an 11–25 km global grid.
